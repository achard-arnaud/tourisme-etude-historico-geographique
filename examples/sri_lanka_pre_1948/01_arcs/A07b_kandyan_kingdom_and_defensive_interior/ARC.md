## Problem-first causal question

**Why does the same interior geography first make Kandy unusually costly to conquer, then fail to prevent the political loss of sovereignty in 1815, and finally become an asset for a British plantation economy?**

This is a form-global question. The arc must narrate events and local mechanisms before stating the wider consequence. The geography is not scenery: it changes transport, supply, surveillance, coalition options and later crop/export economics.

## Causal spine

The arc tests a sign change in the value of the highlands:

**difficult relief + narrow approaches + supply vulnerability → defensive depth**

then

**political fracture + improved imperial information/coalition + reduced external balancing options → negotiated transfer of sovereignty in 1815**

then

**post-rebellion road + 1833 administrative unification + plantation capital + railway → lower transport/control cost → highlands become an export-producing interior.**

The key analytical point is that the terrain does not cease to exist. Its political meaning changes because institutions, infrastructure, capital and the regional balance change.

## Findings closed in Run17–47

- `C-R17-KDY-1815-001` — the kingdom ended in 1815 through the Kandyan Convention after political manoeuvring between British officials and Kandyan chiefs; the record rejects a simple final battlefield-conquest story.
- `C-R17-KDY-ADMIN-001` — Colebrooke–Cameron reforms in 1833 reorganized administration and justice on an island-wide basis, further dismantling the former separate Kandyan institutional order.
- `C-R17-KDY-PLANTATION-001` — the Colombo–Kandy road belongs to both territorial control and plantation-opening sequences; it must not be reduced to a single-purpose coffee road.
- `C-R17-KDY-RAIL-001` — rail was introduced in 1864 with the explicit institutional purpose of carrying hill-country coffee to Colombo; the line reached Kandy in 1867.
- `C-R17-KDY-LABOUR-001` — plantation expansion relied on large-scale South-Indian labour immigration; this is analytically distinct from the prior appropriation/alienation of Kandyan highland land.
- Run44 correction — the transition from VOC cinnamon to British coffee/tea is not a simple reuse of the same land. The continuity is export-oriented and institutional; the productive geography shifts strongly toward the highlands.

## Field observation and defensive mechanism

Field observation from Kandy (2026-08-26): the present urban core does not read like a bastioned European fortress despite repeated colonial invasions.

The supported explanation is a **defence in depth** rather than absence of defence. Sources document strategic approaches, commanding heights, difficult retreats, disrupted communications and supply, climate/disease and the ability of Kandyan forces to exploit terrain. This supports a crucial distinction:

**entering or temporarily occupying Kandy ≠ conquering and governing the Kandyan kingdom.**

The terrain increases the cost of turning a campaign into durable sovereignty.

## Unresolved but attractive hypothesis

`C-R17-KDY-PASSES-001` remains unresolved: the corpus does **not** establish that the Kandyan state deliberately kept forests, bridges, passes and approaches underdeveloped as a formal defence doctrine. The narrative may say that difficult approaches were used defensively; it may not promote deliberate non-development into settled policy without a documentary anchor.

## 360 viewpoints

### Kandyan monarchy / chiefs

The crown seeks to preserve sovereignty and legitimacy; chiefs possess their own interests and can cooperate, defect or bargain. The same decentralised coalition that can complicate foreign occupation can also make the monarchy politically vulnerable when the ruler loses elite support.

### British power

The early problem is not simply to win a battle but to reduce the cost and uncertainty of governing the interior. Military force, political intelligence, alliances with chiefs, post-rebellion infrastructure and administrative reform are successive instruments for solving different parts of that problem.

### Intermediaries

D'Oyly is the clearest narrative carrier: language, knowledge of institutions and elite contacts reduce political opacity. This does not make him the sole cause of 1815; it shows that information becomes a technology of conquest alongside roads and troops.

### Highland cultivators and plantation workers

Two mechanisms must remain separate. Kandyan land changes under colonial property/plantation expansion; later estates mobilise large-scale South-Indian labour. Do not collapse these groups into one undifferentiated story of dispossession.

## Event before dezoom: 1796 → 1815

The narrative order is mandatory:

1. Britain takes the former Dutch maritime possessions in 1795–1796.
2. Kandy remains sovereign.
3. Direct military penetration still encounters the old logistics/terrain problem; the 1803 experience shows that occupation of the capital is not enough.
4. British officials deepen political intelligence and relations with Kandyan chiefs.
5. Conflict between Sri Vikrama Rajasinha and important elites creates a coalition opportunity.
6. The 1815 Convention transfers sovereignty.
7. The 1817–1818 rebellion demonstrates that elite agreement did not equal social pacification.
8. **Only after this sequence** should the narrative dezoom to the wider contraction of Dutch/French balancing options and British fiscal-military asymmetry in the Indian Ocean.

## ROI of sovereignty

The useful heuristic is qualitative:

**strategic/economic value of the interior versus cost of coercion + supply + garrison + administration + rebellion.**

For Portuguese and VOC regimes, the interior often offered poor returns relative to the cost of permanent control when coastal rents could be captured without annexation. Under Britain, the equation changes through larger imperial resources, administrative integration and later plantation/infrastructure value.

Do not convert this into invented numerical profitability.

## Infrastructure reverses the geography

The post-conquest sequence is the arc's central geographic payoff:

- road reduces the cost of military/administrative penetration and also opens investment/land access;
- 1833 reduces institutional separation between former maritime and Kandyan territories;
- coffee turns hill land into an export asset;
- rail explicitly lowers the transport constraint between Kandy district plantations and Colombo;
- the same mountain system that once created defensive depth becomes an organised hinterland of the colonial port economy.

Formula for the reader:

**la montagne qui augmentait le coût de l’empire devient, après infrastructure, une ressource de l’empire.**

## Guardrails

- Do not claim terrain alone explains Kandyan survival.
- Do not claim 1815 was a straightforward military conquest.
- Do not infer formal deliberate underdevelopment of passes/forests from defensive effectiveness.
- Do not collapse 1815 political takeover, 1817–18 pacification, 1833 administrative unification, land alienation, plantation investment and labour migration into one event.
- Do not narrate cinnamon→coffee→tea as the same land/labour regime.
- Do not explain the railway as solely military; the institutional source explicitly anchors coffee transport.

## Exit rupture / bridge forward

By the late nineteenth century the coast/interior opposition has changed sign. The interior is no longer primarily a sovereign refuge outside the maritime state: it is increasingly connected to Colombo by administration, roads, rail and export agriculture. That transformation prepares the later problem of what an independent state inherits from a territorial and infrastructural order built for colonial control and world-market integration.
