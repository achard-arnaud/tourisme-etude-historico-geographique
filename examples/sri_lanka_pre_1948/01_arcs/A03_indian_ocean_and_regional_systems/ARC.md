## Entry rupture

A02 establishes that an inland Lankan polity could reach several maritime facades. A03 changes scale: the question is no longer only **which port exists**, but **what the wider monsoon system makes possible or costly for rulers, merchants and communities using those ports**.

## Causal problem

**How did seasonal navigation and overlapping western/eastern Indian Ocean networks change the options available to Sri Lankan ports and rulers — and how did those changing networks alter the value of places such as Polonnaruwa without mechanically determining political change?**

## Causal spine

Indian Ocean navigation was structured by seasonality rather than continuous timetable-like movement. Merchants and crews could have to wait for return winds, making prolonged residence, brokerage and trust networks economically useful. This mechanism helps explain how trading diasporas could become durable communities in ports without requiring a single founding migration. `C-R45-MONSOON-MERCHANT-DIASPORA-001` remains the strongest mechanism connecting the maritime map to the later history of Muslim merchant communities.

Sri Lanka's position mattered because several ports faced different parts of this system. Mantai/Mahātittha linked the north-west strongly to South India and the western Indian Ocean while remaining connected to longer eastward exchange. Gokanna/Trincomalee opened toward the Bay of Bengal. Other northern and southern gateways changed the available combinations of cabotage, transshipment, pilgrimage and diplomacy. The island is therefore better described as a **switching zone among overlapping circuits** than as a single stop on one linear Silk Road.

### Gokanna, Mahaweli and Polonnaruwa — contribution, not monocause

RUN49 resolves the strongest version of the Gokanna hypothesis. Official Sri Lankan heritage synthesis establishes that Polonnaruwa was already strategically important during the Anuradhapura period, before becoming the medieval capital. It also treats proximity to the eastern natural harbour and the Mahaweli corridor as important to Polonnaruwa's later foreign relations and movement of merchandise. UNESCO material independently corroborates the long inland corridor linking the Mahaweli basin toward Trincomalee/Koddiyar Bay.

The causal reading is therefore bounded. **East-coast access increased the value of the Polonnaruwa location, but did not by itself cause the capital shift.** Its rise must remain multi-causal: pre-existing strategic status, defensibility, relation to the Mahaweli, agricultural/hydraulic potential, Chola administrative use, dynastic decisions and access to the Bay of Bengal all matter. The interesting historical change is not “trade moves east, therefore the capital moves east,” but that the same location becomes attractive for several reasons that increasingly reinforce one another.

This is a useful example of the project method: geography changes the menu of feasible strategies, but it does not dictate a unique political outcome.

### Dhow / outrigger panel — illustration, not dated frontier

The National Museum's `Ancient Sea Routes` panel remains valuable because it juxtaposes a `Dhow Technology Zone` and an `Outrigger Technology Zone` and places Sri Lanka amid routes arriving from both western and eastern sectors of the ocean. Its source is securely identified as _Sri Lanka and the Silk Road of the Sea_ (Bandaranayake et al., 1990).

RUN49 closes the chronology question negatively within scope. Accessible nautical scholarship documents a living Sri Lankan outrigger tradition and long-distance Austronesian navigation across the Indian Ocean, but it does not supply a defensible date for the static boundaries drawn on the museum panel. Modern nautical work also cautions against retro-reading classical descriptions as direct proof of outriggers. The panel must therefore remain **illustration-only for the technology zones**.

The supported proposition is narrower and stronger: Sri Lanka participated in maritime systems connected both westward and eastward, and different nautical traditions circulated through the wider Indian Ocean. The unsupported proposition is that the island was a uniquely identifiable, dateable frontier where two exclusive technological civilizations met.

## Early-modern rupture

This regional system changes again in the early modern period. Portuguese naval coercion does not create the spice routes it encounters; it attempts to redirect and police parts of an older network through armed shipping, passes, forts and monopoly claims. Later VOC and European bullion flows operate on a maritime field already structured by Asian merchants and intra-Asian commerce. These claims belong in A03 because they show a change in the **rules and coercive capacity of the network**, while remaining chronologically separated from the ancient museum panel.

## 360 viewpoints

- **Port merchants and crews:** seasonal winds create waiting time, residence, brokerage and incentives for durable trust networks.
- **Lankan rulers:** a multi-port island widens diplomatic and commercial options; Polonnaruwa's eastern access becomes one strategic asset among several.
- **Resident/intermediary communities:** repeated commercial residence can become settlement, marriage, religious institution and social reproduction.
- **External maritime powers:** Sri Lanka's value lies partly in plugging into or coercively redirecting an existing network, rather than discovering an unknown route.

## Illustration logic

`ILL-R36-ANCIENT-SEA-ROUTES-02` remains `vision_validated`. It should illustrate route intersection and contact among maritime traditions, never exact technology-zone chronology.

## Guardrails

- Do not describe Sri Lanka as the unique or exact geographic point where “the dhow world” and “the Austronesian world” met.
- Do not treat dhow/outrigger zones as ethnic frontiers or as static across a millennium.
- Do not infer a single Arab founding migration from western Indian Ocean routes.
- Do not use Gokanna as a monocausal explanation of Polonnaruwa's rise.
- Do not collapse ancient navigation, medieval Muslim commerce and early-modern European coercion into one undifferentiated route map.

## Exit rupture / bridge forward

The relevant long-duration change is not “Sri Lanka enters world trade” — it was connected much earlier. The next rupture is **who can shape the terms of access to those networks**: merchant diasporas, South Indian powers, Lankan rulers, then armed European companies. That is the bridge from connectivity to coercion, sovereignty and colonial port systems.
