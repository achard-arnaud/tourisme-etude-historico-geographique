# A03 — Indian Ocean and regional systems

## Entry rupture

A02 establishes that an inland Lankan polity could reach several maritime facades. A03 changes scale: the question is no longer only **which port exists**, but **what the wider monsoon system makes possible or costly for rulers, merchants and communities using those ports**.

## Causal problem

**How did seasonal navigation and overlapping western/eastern Indian Ocean networks change the options available to Sri Lankan ports and rulers — and when does a useful regional dezoom become an overconfident claim about routes, technologies or ethnic origins?**

## Causal spine

Indian Ocean navigation was structured by seasonality rather than continuous timetable-like movement. Merchants and crews could have to wait for return winds, making prolonged residence, brokerage and trust networks economically useful. This mechanism helps explain how trading diasporas could become durable communities in ports without requiring a single founding migration. `C-R45-MONSOON-MERCHANT-DIASPORA-001` is the strongest current mechanism connecting the maritime map to the later history of Muslim merchant communities.

Sri Lanka's position mattered because several of its ports faced different parts of this system. Mantai/Mahātittha strongly linked the north-west to South India and the western Indian Ocean while remaining part of longer eastward exchange. Gokanna/Trincomalee opened toward the Bay of Bengal. Other northern and southern gateways changed the available combinations of cabotage, transshipment, pilgrimage and diplomacy. The island is therefore better described as a **switching zone among overlapping circuits** than as a single stop on one linear Silk Road.

The National Museum's `Ancient Sea Routes` panel adds a useful visual hypothesis by juxtaposing a `Dhow Technology Zone` and an `Outrigger Technology Zone`. Its source has now been identified as _Sri Lanka and the Silk Road of the Sea_ (Bandaranayake et al., 1990). Independent scholarship strongly supports Austronesian long-distance navigation across the Indian Ocean and the Madagascar connection, while traditional outrigger forms are documented around Sri Lanka and South India. That evidence is enough to make the map useful as a **contact-zone illustration**.

It is not enough to turn the drawn boundaries into a dated technological frontier. `Dhow` is a broad label for heterogeneous western Indian Ocean craft traditions; outrigger technologies also diffused and changed over long periods. The precise chronology and evidentiary basis of the two museum zones therefore remain open in `Q-R48-DHOW-OUTRIGGER-ZONE-CHRONOLOGY-001`.

This regional system changes again in the early modern period. Portuguese naval coercion does not create the spice routes it encounters; it attempts to redirect and police parts of an older network through armed shipping, passes, forts and monopoly claims. Later VOC and European bullion flows operate on a maritime field already structured by Asian merchants and intra-Asian commerce. These early-modern claims belong in A03 because they show a change in the **rules and coercive capacity of the network**, but they must remain chronologically separated from the ancient dhow/outrigger panel.

## 360 viewpoints

- **Port merchants and crews:** seasonal winds create waiting time, residence, brokerage and incentives for durable trust networks.
- **Lankan rulers and institutions:** access to more than one facade widens diplomatic and commercial options but does not guarantee direct control over overseas networks.
- **Resident/intermediary communities:** repeated commercial residence can become settlement, marriage, religious institution and social reproduction.
- **External maritime powers:** the value of Sri Lanka lies partly in plugging into or coercively redirecting an existing network, rather than discovering an unknown route.

Use only the viewpoints that are evidenced in the period being narrated; this matrix is not a licence to project one community unchanged across centuries.

## Illustration logic

`ILL-R36-ANCIENT-SEA-ROUTES-02` is now `vision_validated`, with the 1990 source identified and the technology-zone limitations explicit. It should illustrate route intersection and technological contact, not prove exact route chronology or an exclusive meeting of two maritime civilizations.

A modern CC0 map derived from Manguin's work on Austronesian maritime networks is retained as a research comparator. It should not replace the field photograph automatically because it answers a narrower question and lacks the museum panel's juxtaposition of western and eastern technology zones.

## Guardrails

- Do not describe Sri Lanka as the unique or exact geographic point where “the dhow world” and “the Austronesian world” met.
- Do not treat dhow/outrigger zones as ethnic frontiers or as static across a millennium.
- Do not infer a single Arab founding migration from the existence of western Indian Ocean routes.
- Do not turn monsoon seasonality into a claim that every merchant had to settle permanently.
- Do not collapse ancient/early historic navigation, medieval Muslim commerce and early-modern European coercion into one undifferentiated route map.
- Do not use the museum panel as primary proof of the precise chronology of vessel technology diffusion.

## Open mechanism tests

- `Q-R48-GOKANNA-POLONNARUWA-CAUSALITY-001` — test whether changing Bay of Bengal/Gokanna access materially influenced the shift of political optimum toward Polonnaruwa, rather than merely benefiting from it.

## Exit rupture / bridge forward

The relevant long-duration change is not “Sri Lanka enters world trade” — it was connected much earlier. The next rupture is **who can shape the terms of access to those networks**: merchant diasporas, South Indian powers, Lankan rulers, then armed European companies. That is the bridge from connectivity to coercion, sovereignty and colonial port systems.
