# Problem-first causal dezoom contract — Run46

This companion contract is mandatory for advanced analytical historical-travel narration.

## Why this exists

The existing storytelling pipeline is strong on evidence conservation, core-before-side-story ordering, placement and uncertainty. It is weaker on **how the core itself is framed**. A chronologically correct core can still read like a sequence of facts if it does not explicitly organize evidence around a problem, a mechanism and a change of scale.

Run46 adds that missing layer without changing evidence thresholds.

## 1. Problem before chronology

Each major chapter/subchapter must begin from a bounded analytical problem that can be answered by the available graph.

Good examples:
- How can a maritime empire control an island without holding its interior?
- Why does a mountain refuge become the centre of a plantation economy a century later?
- Why does a lagoon port matter if it is not a deep-water naval harbour?
- What survives socially when a colonial regime disappears politically?

The problem is an editorial frame, not evidence. It must not imply a causal answer that the graph cannot support.

## 2. Evidence before consequence

Narration order is mandatory when a causal dezoom is used:

1. **scene / event / object / sequence** — what happened or what is observed;
2. **local mechanism** — why actors could or could not act as they did;
3. **consequence** — what changed locally;
4. **dezoom causal** — only then move to the wider imperial, oceanic, social or economic system;
5. **return to terrain / next rupture** — show what becomes visible afterward.

Never open with the abstract consequence and then backfill events merely as examples.

## 3. Graph-to-problem derivation

Before drafting, inspect the relevant claim/bridge neighborhood and identify:
- the main dependent variable: what changed?
- competing mechanisms or confounders;
- spatial scale where the mechanism operates;
- actors whose incentives differ;
- the unresolved question that makes the section worth reading.

Use bridges to formulate the problem, but never render bridge metadata in prose.

## 4. Core versus side-story gate

A fragment belongs in the **core** when removing it would break explanation of:
- mobilisation or coercion;
- legitimacy / coalition;
- governing or defence cost;
- flows / opportunity;
- social reproduction;
- regime or centre change;
- the geographic mechanism that makes one of the above possible.

Otherwise type it as a side story using the existing side-story profiles.

Do not demote an analytically necessary causal dezoom into a side story merely because it changes scale.

## 5. Side-story typing after core explanation

When material is genuinely off-trunk, type it before drafting:
- `detour`: autonomous historical episode that illuminates the host mechanism;
- `dezoom`: bounded excursion to a higher geographic/systemic scale, then explicit return;
- `portrait`: person/community as carrier of a larger mechanism;
- `also`: adjacent case that expands without becoming a comparator claim;
- `method`: epistemic or source-reading explanation.

A dezoom must never substitute for the core causal chain.

## 6. 360 viewpoint matrix

For major regime transitions, test at least four viewpoints where evidence exists:
- external ruler/company/state objective;
- local sovereign/elite strategy;
- intermediary incentives;
- affected producers/workers/communities.

Do not mechanically render all four. Use the viewpoints that change the explanation.

The goal is not moral balance or symmetrical word count. It is to expose different constraints, gains, losses and information sets.

## 7. Geography must do causal work

Do not describe geography as scenery. Ask what it changes in:
- transport cost;
- visibility / surveillance;
- defence depth;
- seasonality;
- access to ports / passes / rivers / lagoons;
- crop suitability;
- ability to tax or monopolize;
- ability to escape, migrate, smuggle or ally.

A geographic statement is trunk-worthy only when it changes an actor's feasible options or the cost/benefit of control.

## 8. Port-function rule

Do not narrate colonial ports as interchangeable dots on a fort map. Classify the period-specific function first, allowing overlaps:
- trans-strait interface;
- extractive/export-administrative port;
- naval-strategic port;
- lagoon/internal-interface port;
- pilgrimage/religious gateway;
- seasonal resource port.

Then explain why the fort, tax office, market or route is located there.

## 9. Intent → instrument → friction → adaptation → unintended effect

For colonial or state interventions, prefer this causal sequence when supported:

**intent → instrument → friction → adaptation → unintended effect**

Examples of friction:
- mountain logistics;
- monsoon seasonality;
- smuggling;
- local elite resistance;
- labour flight;
- competing imperial power;
- administrative information failure.

This pattern is especially useful for showing why a policy can strengthen an adversary, rigidify a category, create a new intermediary class or generate infrastructure with later uses not intended by its builder.

## 10. Successive-regime inheritance

When regimes change, explicitly test what is:
- destroyed;
- retained;
- repurposed;
- expanded;
- socially reproduced;
- renamed/reclassified.

Objects of inheritance include forts, law, registers, tax categories, communities, language skills, religious institutions, labour systems, roads, ports and commercial routes.

Never imply total institutional replacement without evidence.

## 11. ROI-of-sovereignty heuristic

For expansion/conquest questions, test qualitatively:

**expected revenue/strategic value versus cost of coercion, garrison, transport, administration and rebellion.**

This is an analytical heuristic, not a licence to fabricate quantitative profitability.

It is particularly useful when explaining why the same territory can be unattractive under one regime and valuable under another after technology, crop demand or infrastructure changes.

## 12. Iterative storytelling pass

For iterative mode, before adding new prose:
1. identify the last stable narrative baseline;
2. list all eligible new claims, bridges, intakes, questions, illustrations and side stories since that baseline;
3. group them by reader problem, not run number;
4. repair the chronological core first;
5. insert causal dezooms where the event sequence now supports them;
6. type and place only genuinely off-trunk side stories;
7. account for every eligible fragment with an explicit disposition.

Run numbers are provenance, never reader structure.

## 13. QA additions

A Run46-style narrative fails review if:
- a chapter begins with a conclusion that has not yet been narrated;
- a causal dezoom appears before the event/local mechanism it explains;
- a core geographic mechanism was demoted to side-story status;
- successive colonial regimes are rendered as full replacements where inheritance is known;
- a port is described only as a fort location without its functional geography;
- 360 viewpoint language invents motives or treats all actors as homogeneous;
- unintended effects are asserted without a documented instrument and plausible transmission channel.
