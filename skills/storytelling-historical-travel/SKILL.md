# Storytelling historical travel

## Reader contract
Before rendering, resolve or infer five fields and keep them stable across the output:
- **audience**: advanced / intermediate / child;
- **language**: output language and translation policy for names/terms;
- **tone**: analytical, guide-like, narrative, contemplative, etc.;
- **register**: scholarly, educated generalist, family/child;
- **length budget**: total size and reading context (on-site, train, evening, flight, reference).

If the user has already established these preferences, do not ask again.

## Audience presets
### advanced
Preserve historiographic disputes, named scholars/source families, competing causal models and explicit uncertainty. Assume the reader can retain dynasties, institutions and cross-arc references. Explain only specialist terms that are necessary to the mechanism.

### intermediate
Keep the causal architecture but reduce proper-name density. Explain technical terms inline, prefer one canonical example per mechanism, and surface controversies only when they change the interpretation.

### child
Use concrete places, objects and human-scale stakes. Prefer short causal chains and scene-led openings. A more novelistic rhythm is allowed, but **never invent dialogue, thoughts, motives, dates or events**. Mark uncertainty simply (“on ne sait pas exactement…”). Avoid good-guys/bad-guys framing and preserve agency on all sides.

## Length budget
Allocate words by causal weight, not equal chapter size. Default bands:
- quick: 2,000–5,000 words;
- standard: 8,000–15,000;
- long: 20,000–35,000;
- monumental: 40,000+.

Within the budget, protect the causal spine first; compress lists, repeated biographies and decorative detail before cutting mechanisms or transitions. A site-specific detour may be long if the reader is physically there and it activates several later arcs.

## Narrative unit
For each arc prefer:
1. a location, object, map or concrete tension as hook;
2. the causal question;
3. two to four mechanisms;
4. consequence for people, territory and institutions;
5. a forward bridge explaining why the next arc becomes possible.

Do not force this template when it would sound mechanical.

## Cross-reference policy
Use a **cross-reference** only when it saves repetition or activates causality. Prefer natural callbacks (“on l’a vu à Polonnaruwa…”) and forward signals (“cela deviendra décisif sous la VOC…”). Do not build a hyperlink forest or require the reader to consult annexes. Reintroduce an entity briefly after a long gap.

## Side material
Integrate useful non-trunk material as `Mais aussi`, `Petit détour`, `Point de méthode`, or `Fausse piste`. A side box must either deepen a mechanism, correct an intuitive misconception, or reward the site being visited. Otherwise delete it.

## Density and reference control
Advanced mode may cite source families in prose. Intermediate mode moves most source mechanics to notes/end-of-section references. Child mode normally omits apparatus from the story but keeps a parent/teacher source note outside the narrative.

Avoid more than 3–5 new named entities in one paragraph unless the list itself is the subject. Introduce a dynasty, institution or place before using it as shorthand later.

## Story QA
Before delivery verify:
- every section answers “why does this matter?”;
- chronology remains recoverable even when using flashbacks;
- vivid reconstruction is anchored and never fictionalized as fact;
- cross-references reduce repetition rather than create dependency;
- length budget is respected within roughly ±10% unless the user requests flexibility;
- the conclusion returns to the main causal map rather than merely recapping events.
