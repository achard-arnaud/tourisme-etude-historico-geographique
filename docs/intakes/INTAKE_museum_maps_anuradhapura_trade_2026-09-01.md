# Intake — museum maps: Anuradhapura-period spatial system and ancient sea routes

## Context

Field visit, 2026-09-01. Two user-supplied photographs document museum cartography in Sri Lanka.

- Photo 1: island-scale map titled **Sri Lanka — Anuradhapura Period**. The legend visibly distinguishes canals, sub-capitals, monastery sites, ancient ports and other urban/political sites over a hydrographic and topographic base.
- Photo 2: panel titled **TRADE / Ancient Sea Routes**. The legend distinguishes **Major Sea Routes**, **Dhow Technology Zone** and **Outrigger Technology Zone**.

The photographs are valuable primarily as evidence of a modern museum's explanatory model. They are **not primary evidence for ancient routes, political boundaries or vessel technology**. Historical propositions derived from them must therefore be corroborated independently.

## Immediate interpretation

### 1. Anuradhapura-period map

The important feature is not any one dot but the spatial logic. The map combines:

1. an inland royal and monastic core in the north-central dry zone;
2. a dense water-management layer of rivers, tanks and canals;
3. secondary centres and monasteries distributed through the hinterland;
4. ports on several coasts, with particularly important north-western access toward Mannar/Mantai;
5. the central highlands as a major topographic discontinuity rather than the demographic/political heart of the Anuradhapura system.

This supports a reading of Anuradhapura not as an isolated inland capital but as the centre of a **coupled agrarian–hydraulic–religious–commercial system**. The dry-zone political economy depended on stored and redirected water, while coastal ports connected the inland surplus-producing and monastic landscape to wider Indian Ocean exchange.

### 2. Ancient sea-routes map

The second panel depicts Sri Lanka close to the crossing point between western Indian Ocean circuits, the Indian subcontinent and Bay of Bengal / Southeast Asian routes. Its strongest historical message is therefore geographic: the island lies near a place where routes can branch north toward India, west toward the Arabian Sea and Red Sea/Persian Gulf sphere, and east toward Southeast Asia and China.

The panel's **dhow** and **outrigger** zones should be treated cautiously. They are broad museum-level technological distributions, not precise chronological borders. The term “dhow” itself groups several western Indian Ocean vessel traditions, while outrigger technology has a deep Austronesian/Southeast Asian history and diffuse transfer zones. No claim about a precise vessel type, date or exclusive cultural boundary is promoted from this map alone.

## Bridges created by this intake

### Anuradhapura ↔ Mantai/Mannar

Modern archaeology strongly supports Mantai as a major entrepôt and gateway between the north-west coast and the Anuradhapura interior. Its position between the Arabian Sea, Palk Strait, Bay of Bengal and routes around southern Sri Lanka helps explain why an inland capital could participate deeply in maritime exchange.

### Hydraulic surplus ↔ ports ↔ monasteries

The maps together make visible a useful system chain:

**monsoon water capture → irrigated rice and settlement density → royal/monastic institutions → inland transport corridors → ports → Indian Ocean exchange → imported goods, ideas and prestige resources.**

This must not be simplified into a single centrally controlled “hydraulic state”: archaeological work on the Anuradhapura hinterland instead indicates a mixed landscape in which monasteries, local communities and elites all participated in land and water management.

### Polonnaruwa continuity

The maps also improve the Anuradhapura→Polonnaruwa story. The shift of capital in the eleventh century changed the political centre but did not suddenly create Sri Lanka's maritime connectivity. The older Anuradhapura system had already linked inland power, monasteries, irrigation and ports for centuries. Polonnaruwa inherited and reconfigured a pre-existing island-wide and Indian Ocean geography.

## Epistemic limits

- Museum map lines are schematic routes, not reconstructed GPS tracks.
- “Anuradhapura Period” compresses more than a millennium of political and infrastructural change.
- A dot on the map does not prove continuous occupation or equal importance throughout the whole period.
- Technology-zone boundaries are interpretive and potentially anachronistic when detached from a date range.
- The two images are best used as illustrations and research triggers; promoted claims below rely on independent archaeological sources.
