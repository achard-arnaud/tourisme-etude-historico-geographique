# Source policy

## Two independent axes
Tier sources by **epistemic role**, not brand prestige:
- T0 — primary/material evidence: inscription, archive, object, excavation, law, contemporaneous record;
- T1 — academic research: peer-reviewed or scholarly monograph/article with method and references;
- T2 — institutional synthesis: archaeology departments, UNESCO/ICOMOS, museums, foundations, curated exhibitions, public research programmes;
- T3 — navigation/encyclopedia: orientation, chronology discovery and terminology;
- T4 — field mediation: panel, guide, oral explanation, tourism interpretation;
- T5 — exploratory lead: weak, derivative or unverified discovery source.

Separately assign an **anchor role**:
- canonical anchor — foundational evidence or research for a claim family;
- specialist institutional anchor — T2 synthesis with demonstrable specialist authorship, institutional collaboration, provenance and narrow subject expertise;
- corroborating bridge — source used to connect two anchored facts or mechanisms;
- lead — source that opens a question but cannot close it.

A **specialist institutional anchor does not become T1** merely because it is prestigious or co-produced by public institutions. The second axis answers “how useful is this source for this claim?”; the tier answers “what kind of knowledge production is this?”.

## Cross-check rule
A causal anchor should normally combine independent source roles. A curated exhibition can be a first-rate map of the problem while still requiring T0/T1 corroboration for contested causal claims, quantitative statements or rhetoric such as “all trade was excluded”. Record contradictions rather than averaging them away.

## Field-to-source promotion
When a field panel points to an identifiable curated corpus, keep the photo/panel as T4 evidence and register the underlying exhibition as T2. If the exhibition cites archives or scholarship, follow those references when the claim is load-bearing.

Sanitization precedes tiering: propaganda, chronicles and colonial records remain valuable evidence about worldview even when their factual claims require corroboration.
